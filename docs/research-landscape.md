# Chasing small edges across many stocks

The 2026 landscape makes it entirely feasible for a solo developer to stand up a working, cost-transparent, paper-tradeable AI-assisted trading spike within days: Alpaca's free, fractional-share-native API paired with zero-commission paper trading removes almost every barrier that used to gate this kind of experiment, and FINRA's June 4, 2026 replacement of the 25-year-old $25,000 Pattern Day Trader rule with a lighter intraday-margin framework removes the other major obstacle for small accounts ([FINRA Regulatory Notice 26-10](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/26-10)). But the honest research verdict is that no signal category available to a retail developer — classical technical indicators, ML-engineered price/volume features, or news and social sentiment — has been shown to produce a real, cost-adjusted edge without heavy walk-forward validation and overfitting correction; most published backtests in this space, including some reporting Sharpe ratios above 3, explicitly omit transaction costs and market impact ([Sentiment trading with large language models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19245)). Technical indicators are the best-documented dead end: a 2024 study running 13 ML models on SPY minute data found directional accuracy collapsing from 80–86% in-sample to 47–49% out-of-sample — worse than a coin flip — while 12 of 13 models underperformed buy-and-hold ([arXiv 2412.15448](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15448v1)). News and sentiment signals show a narrower, more interesting asymmetry: same-day reaction is already captured by high-frequency trading within roughly 250 milliseconds ([The Random Walk of High Frequency Trading](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.3650)), but next-day-to-week drift — the post-earnings-announcement-drift effect, "the most studied anomaly in empirical finance" — has over 55 years of replication behind it and appears concentrated in small, thinly covered stocks ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/post-earnings-announcement-effect)), at the cost of overlapping with real pump-and-dump manipulation risk in exactly those names ([Bloomberg 2026 investigation](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-wall-street-apparent-pump-and-dump-investor-scam/)). For a solo developer, the realistic path is to treat this as a research exercise rather than an income plan: build on Alpaca, source historical data from a mix of free bundled feeds and modestly-priced providers to avoid survivorship bias, and hold any candidate strategy to a walk-forward-validated, cost-and-slippage-adjusted, statistically-corrected bar before it ever touches a paper account — let alone real money.

## Alpaca anchors a friction-free stack; every alternative has a catch

Six brokers dominate the solo-developer conversation in 2026, and they sort cleanly by how much friction they add to an AI-assisted PoC. **Alpaca** is the consistent low-friction leader: same-day KYC, no minimum deposit, a modern official Python SDK (`alpaca-py`), native fractional shares down to $1 notional in both live and paper accounts, and bundled free historical data and news — described in one review as "the recommended starting point for developers new to algo trading APIs" ([AlgoPlatforms Alpaca Review](https://algoplatforms.com/broker-apis/alpaca-api); [Alpaca Fractional Trading docs](https://docs.alpaca.markets/us/docs/fractional-trading)). **Schwab** is a credible second: its Trader API is bundled free with any brokerage account, supports individual (non-company) developer registration, and streams real-time Level 1/Level 2 data at no extra cost — contrasted against "many platforms" that reportedly charge $50–200/month for API access — but it requires OAuth2 setup and carries a more traditional compliance posture ([Schwab Developer Portal](https://developer.schwab.com/); [Medium: Why Charles Schwab API](https://medium.com/@avetik.babayan/why-charles-schwab-api-choosing-the-right-trading-platform-for-automation-bot-6bf6a687bb83)). **Interactive Brokers** offers the single most realistic paper environment — the same order-matching engine as production, $1,000,000 simulated cash, real-time data — but its own documentation cautions that "the Paper Trading Environment relies on more simulated technologies than the Live trading environment," its fractional-share support via API is genuinely contested between IBKR's own conflicting documentation ("not via API at this time" versus community reports of cash-quantity orders working), and IBKR Pro's per-share commission structure ($0.0005–$0.005/share) makes it the most expensive of the group for a high-turnover, many-small-positions strategy ([IBKR Paper Trading docs](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/docs/tws-api/doc/notes-limitations/limitations/paper-trading); [IBKR Fractional Shares Trading Lesson](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/campus/trading-lessons/fractional-shares/)). **Tradier** and **Webull** are viable but narrower: Tradier has no fractional-share support at all via API or web, ruling it out for a "small dollar amounts spread across many names" design, while Webull restricts fractional orders to market-only with a $5 minimum notional ([PickMyTrade Tradier FAQ](https://pickmytrade.io/broker-faq/tradier-faq/); [Webull API Trading Overview](https://developer.webull.com/apis/docs/trade-api/overview/)). **Robinhood** is effectively out of scope for a conventional trading API — it has no documented general-purpose stock/options API, only a crypto API and a newly launched, architecturally different "Agentic Trading" product that connects an AI agent to a Robinhood account via a pasted MCP-config URL, too new for any independent developer track record to exist yet ([Robinhood Newsroom: Open to Agents](https://robinhood.com/us/en/newsroom/robinhood-is-now-open-to-agents/)). Layered on top of the broker landscape, the regulatory environment just shifted in a way that specifically favors small accounts: the old PDT rule's $25,000-or-be-frozen threshold is gone, replaced by a 25%-of-intraday-position-value margin-equity requirement that has no dollar floor ([FINRA Insights: Intraday Margin Requirements](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/intraday-margin-requirements)), and none of it — old or new — applies to paper trading at all, since "a paper account has no regulator" ([tradingsim.com, search-aggregated](https://www.finra.org/investors/insights/intraday-margin-requirements)). The data ecosystem has also consolidated: IEX Cloud, once a popular cheap tier, shut down entirely in August 2024 with no official successor, and Polygon.io rebranded to Massive.com in 2026 without changing its API — two reminders that this ecosystem is still young enough that a PoC's data plumbing should not assume permanence.

## Canadian retail clients cannot API-trade Canadian-listed securities — this is a real, current rule, not forum folklore

This project's non-goals originally flagged Canadian/IIROC regulation as unresearched; it no longer is. Automated order submission by retail clients isn't banned across Canadian markets generally, but it is banned in the exact channel any solo developer would use: an **order-execution-only (OEO)** account — the self-directed, non-advised account type every DIY trader opens, with no broker recommending trades — is barred by CIRO's consolidated **IDPC Rule 3200** (the current form of the former IIROC Dealer Member Rule 3200, present in the rulebook as recently as its December 2024 edition) from accepting orders that a client's own automated order system generated, for orders sent to **a Canadian marketplace** ([CIRO IDPC Rules, Dec 2024](https://www.ciro.ca/sites/default/files/2024-12/IDPC-Rules-123124-EN.pdf)). This isn't a stale or theoretical reading: Interactive Brokers Canada told multiple users as recently as 2022 that "Canadian clients cannot place API orders for Canadian securities," citing this exact rule, and an independent reproduction confirmed it directly — an API order for TSX-listed VCN was rejected with "API/CTCI orders for canadian stocks are not allowed," while the identical API call placing an order for Nasdaq-listed AAPL went through without issue, in the same account ([QuantConnect forum thread](https://www.quantconnect.com/forum/discussion/7303/canadians-not-allowed-to-use-interactive-brokers-api-anymore/)). CIRO's broader electronic-trading risk-control framework (UMIR 7.1, requiring risk-management controls around marketplace access and automated order systems) is separate and still active too, but it's the OEO-specific rule above that actually blocks a retail algo ([CIRO Guidance Note GN-URPART7-26-0002](https://www.ciro.ca/newsroom/publications/requirements-notify-ciro-under-electronic-trading-rule-amendments)).

The restriction is scoped to the **marketplace**, not the client's citizenship or the broker: any CIRO-regulated OEO dealer (IBKR Canada, Questrade, etc.) must enforce it for Canadian-listed securities, but the same Canadian client can freely algo-trade US-listed securities (Nasdaq/NYSE) through the same API and the same account. Practically, this settles a question this repo had left open: **an automated (non-paper) phase of this project must trade US-listed equities, not TSX-listed Canadian stocks, regardless of broker** — which already matches every other piece of evidence in this doc (Alpaca's US-only coverage, the PEAD literature's US focus, the S&P-500/Nasdaq-centric data sources). It does not block anything this repo currently does: paper trading has no regulator, IBKR's Canadian retail access is still real and useful for opening an account and trading US securities via API, and Alpaca was never going to touch Canadian-listed stocks anyway. It does mean a *live*, Canadian-brokered, *Canadian-stock* automated strategy is not legally available to a DIY retail developer at all, under any broker — that path doesn't exist, not just "wasn't chosen here."

## The commissions are nearly free; the real costs are data, fees, and settlement lag

"Commission-free" is true but incomplete across every broker in this set — it describes only the headline trade commission, not the full cost stack a solo developer actually pays. Regulatory pass-through fees are universal and unavoidable: the **FINRA Trading Activity Fee** on sells is $0.000195/share (capped at $9.79/trade) as of January 1, 2026, and the **SEC Section 31 fee** on sells is $20.60 per $1,000,000 of principal as of April 2026 — both small in absolute terms for a modest-capital PoC but structurally present on every broker, with FINRA separately proposing a three-month TAF pause from October to December 2026 ([FINRA TAF guidance](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/guidance/trading-activity-fee); [Longbridge SEC fee breakdown](https://longbridge.com/en/academy/us-stocks/blog/a-comprehensive-guide-to-u-s-equity-trading-costs-sec-fees-finra-taf-and-hidden-costs-explained-100537)). The less visible cost is payment for order flow: Robinhood and Webull both route retail orders to market makers like Citadel Securities and Virtu in exchange for PFOF, a practice research continues to associate with worse execution quality — after the UK banned PFOF in 2012, the share of retail trades executing at the best quoted price rose from 65% to over 90% between 2010 and 2014 ([Bookmap PFOF guide](https://bookmap.com/blog/what-is-payment-for-order-flow-pfof)). Interactive Brokers is the one broker in this set not funded by PFOF, which matters if a systematic strategy is sensitive to adverse selection in its fills, though that comes bundled with IBKR's per-share commissions.

| Alpaca | Free | Yes, native, down to $1 | Free (15-min delayed SIP + real-time IEX); $99/mo for full real-time SIP/OPRA | Yes, well-documented |

Data costs follow a similar shape: free tiers are genuinely usable for a daily/weekly-horizon PoC, but each has a specific limitation that bites a "many stocks" design. Alpha Vantage's free tier caps at **25 API requests per day**, unworkable for pulling daily bars across a diversified universe without multi-day batching ([Alpha Vantage pricing](https://www.alphavantage.co/premium/)); `yfinance`, the most commonly used free library, relies on unofficial, undocumented Yahoo endpoints and is subject to active, currently-reported rate-limiting (`YFRateLimitError`) that can silently return partial results under load rather than a clean error ([ranaroussi/yfinance#2411](https://github.com/ranaroussi/yfinance/issues/2411)). Alpaca's free tier is the most practical single source — over 5 years of history across thousands of symbols, with pagination for longer pulls — making it the natural default given it's also the recommended broker. Beyond free tiers, EOD Historical Data's "All World" plan (~€20/month) and Tiingo's lower tiers sit in the realistic $20–100/month solo-developer budget band, while Norgate Data (6/12-month-only billing, ~$150–$800 per term) sits at the higher end and is better suited to a multi-month commitment than a weekend spike ([massive.com/pricing](https://massive.com/pricing); [eodhd.com/pricing](https://eodhd.com/pricing)). The single highest-leverage place to spend budget, if backtest validity matters, is point-in-time index-constituent data: free tools default to "current S&P 500 list," which silently introduces survivorship bias, while EODHD's Fundamentals tier reconstructs index membership back to April 2012 specifically to correct for this ([EODHD S&P 500 constituents](https://eodhd.com/financial-apis-blog/sp-500-historical-constituents-data)). Regulatory friction, meanwhile, is lighter than most 2026-era retail content still describes: the classic PDT rule is gone for margin accounts, and a cash account was never subject to it at all — the actual binding constraint for a fast-turnover, many-stock strategy is **T+1 settlement** (shortened from T+2 in May 2024), which means proceeds from a Monday sale aren't usable again until Tuesday in a cash account, and reusing them earlier risks a "good faith violation" that can trigger a 90-day cash-only restriction after repeated occurrences ([SEC T+1 Investor Bulletin](https://www.sec.gov/resources-for-investors/investor-alerts-bulletins/new-t1-settlement-cycle-what-investors-need-know-investor-bulletin)). Separately, the IRS wash-sale rule disallows claiming a tax loss on any security repurchased within 30 days — a near-unavoidable bookkeeping complication for a strategy that repeatedly trades the same tickers in a taxable account, though it is purely a tax-reporting issue, not a trading restriction, and does not exist at all in a paper account. None of this — PDT-era or post-2026 margin rules, settlement mechanics, or wash sales — constrains a paper-trading engine in any way, since "the balance in a paper trading account is a number in a database, nothing more."

## Technical indicators are largely priced out; sentiment offers a narrower, contested edge

The evidence on classical technical indicators (moving-average crossovers, RSI, MACD, Bollinger Bands) is unusually consistent across independent studies: signals contain statistically detectable information but that information does not survive contact with realistic costs or a buy-and-hold benchmark. A study spanning 878 stocks over 12 years found technical signals "do on average contain information that may be of value in trading," yet strategies built on them "generally underperform a buy-and-hold strategy" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666827025000143)); a separate Lund University analysis concluded flatly that "all technical indicators perform poorly in terms of profitability and predictive power" ([Lund University study](https://lup.lub.lu.se/luur/download?func=downloadFile&recordOId=8905915&fileOId=8905916)). The decay evidence is direct: McLean and Pontiff's study of 97 published return-predicting characteristics found returns roughly **26% lower** after the original sample period and **58% lower** after academic publication, consistent with trading activity arbitraging the anomaly away once it's public ([McLean & Pontiff](https://www.hec.ca/finance/Fichier/McLean.pdf)) — though this decay was not found to be universal outside the U.S. in a 39-market study by Jacobs and Muller, a useful caveat against over-generalizing ([Jacobs & Muller](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X19301618)). ML approaches fare only somewhat better: a peer-reviewed Journal of Finance paper (Kelly, Malamud & Zhou, 2024) provides real theoretical and empirical grounding that highly overparameterized models can improve return prediction relative to simple ones — a genuine counter to the "complexity causes overfitting" intuition ([NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30217/w30217.pdf)) — but the much larger body of practitioner ML-trading writeups shows the opposite pattern: tiny trade counts, single-stock framing, and in-sample metrics reported without correction. One widely-circulated XGBoost case study claimed a 100.81% total return and Sharpe of 2.71 over just 11 trades on a single stock — a textbook overfitting fingerprint ([Medium quant journey](https://medium.com/@fyang1989/my-quant-journey-leveling-up-tsla-predictions-with-xgboost-8a2f76542ccf)). The corrective tools exist precisely because this pattern is endemic: the Deflated Sharpe Ratio and Probability of Backtest Overfitting frameworks (Bailey, López de Prado, and coauthors) adjust a backtested result for the number of trials, skewness, and kurtosis, and their existence as now-standard practice is itself evidence that uncorrected backtest claims in this literature should be treated as inflated by default ([SSRN: Deflated Sharpe Ratio](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551)).

News and sentiment signals split cleanly along the same same-day-versus-drift line the regulatory research surfaced for settlement. Same-day reaction is real but closed: algorithmic systems identify a news article's sentiment and importance within about 0.33 seconds, with price and volume reaction concentrated in the first five seconds, inside a roughly 250-millisecond pricing-inefficiency window that "is not enough time for a human to register prices or execute a trade" ([The Random Walk of High Frequency Trading](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.3650)). The more retail-relevant question is next-day-to-week drift, and here post-earnings-announcement drift — discovered in 1968 and called by Eugene Fama "the granddaddy of underreaction events" — is genuinely the most replicated anomaly in empirical finance ([Quantpedia](https://quantpedia.com/strategies/post-earnings-announcement-effect)). A large-scale 2024 study of 965,375 U.S. news articles found LLM-derived sentiment scores (OPT, a GPT-3-class model) reached 74.4% classification accuracy versus just 50.1% for the traditional Loughran-McDonald finance dictionary, and long-short portfolios built on that sentiment produced backtested Sharpe ratios as high as 3.05 — explicitly without modeling transaction costs, a caveat the report should not let readers forget ([Sentiment trading with large language models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19245)). GPT-4-derived sentiment specifically predicted "subsequent drift, especially for smaller stocks and negative news," concentrating any retail-accessible edge in exactly the less-liquid, less-covered names institutions pay less attention to ([arXiv 2602.00086](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.00086v1)). But social-media sentiment specifically is weaker than headline/earnings sentiment: studies of r/wallstreetbets found "daily sentiment changes alone did not significantly correlate with stock price movements," while simpler attention metrics — comment volume, Google search trends — outperformed sentiment polarity, and at least one study characterized elevated social attention as a *contrarian* predictor of returns ([arXiv 2508.02089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.02089); [Dumb money? ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405918825000212)). This matters because the same small, thinly-covered stocks where genuine drift-based edges are largest are also where a 2026 Bloomberg investigation found a quarter of 250-plus small-tier Nasdaq IPOs since 2023 were promoted in WhatsApp groups before crashing or being suspended by the SEC over suspected manipulation ([Bloomberg investigation](https://www.bloomberg.com/graphics/2026-wall-street-apparent-pump-and-dump-investor-scam/)) — the accessible signal and the manipulated signal occupy substantially the same universe of stocks.

## Ranking what a solo developer can actually build

Five approaches are realistically buildable with 2026 tooling, and they rank differently depending on whether "feasibility" means ease of implementation or credibility of the resulting signal — a distinction this PoC should keep explicit rather than conflate. The most feasible and lowest-risk starting point is not a trading strategy at all but the **research infrastructure itself**: a data pipeline (Alpaca bars plus a survivorship-bias-free constituent list), a backtesting engine (`zipline-reloaded` for research-grade, point-in-time-aware workflows, or `vectorbt` for fast parameter sweeps, given `backtrader` is no longer actively maintained), and a paper-trading harness — all buildable in days with zero signal-quality risk, since it produces no false confidence about profitability. Second, a **multi-asset gradient-boosted or regularized-linear model using engineered price/volume features**, tested with strict walk-forward validation and Deflated-Sharpe correction, is the most defensible signal-bearing category: it is directly supported by peer-reviewed evidence that complexity can add real predictive value at scale ([Kelly, Malamud & Zhou](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w30217/w30217.pdf)), requires less data than deep sequence models, and one hybrid LSTM-XGBoost study specifically found gradient boosting delivers "competitive short-horizon accuracy" — a better fit for a daily/weekly horizon than longer-horizon LSTM strengths ([arXiv 2609.13125](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.13125)). Third, a **lagged earnings/news-sentiment drift strategy on small-to-mid-cap names**, built on Alpaca's free Benzinga-powered News API, has the strongest single piece of supporting academic evidence (55+ years of PEAD literature) but requires an explicit pump-and-dump filter given how much the accessible universe overlaps with manipulation targets. Fourth, **pure classical technical-indicator strategies** (moving-average crossovers, RSI/MACD alone) are the easiest of all to code — a weekend's work — but this is precisely the category with the most consistent evidence of being priced out; building one is worthwhile only as a benchmark to beat, not as a candidate strategy in its own right. Fifth and least feasible is a **real-time social-media sentiment strategy** (Reddit/StockTwits/X): beyond the weak sentiment-polarity signal itself, the 2026 API landscape has become actively hostile to solo developers — X moved to a pay-per-credit model with no free tier as of February 2026, Reddit's commercial tier runs roughly $12,000/month, and StockTwits' official API is frozen pending an internal review that has halted new registrations entirely ([X API 2026](https://www.socialcrawl.dev/blog/x-twitter-api-2026); [Reddit API pricing 2026](https://www.techloy.com/reddit-api-pricing-in-2026-complete-guide-for-developers-and-businesses/); [StockTwits API status](https://apitracker.io/a/stocktwits)). Statistical arbitrage/pairs trading sits outside this ranking as a distinct case: it is mechanically simple to implement and educational, but no source found in this research quantified a realistic retail-achievable return after borrow costs and slippage, and the genuinely profitable form of the strategy is well-documented institutional territory — best treated as a learning exercise, not a PoC candidate with real expected value.

## What the first proof-of-concept should actually test

The first PoC should be framed as a falsifiable hypothesis, not a product: **a gradient-boosted model using engineered price/volume features (20-, 50-day moving-average distance, RSI, realized volatility, and volume-relative-to-average) plus a lagged (T+1) news-sentiment score from Alpaca's Benzinga feed, rebalanced weekly across a diversified basket of 50–100 liquid small-and-mid-cap US equities, produces risk-adjusted returns exceeding a buy-and-hold benchmark of the same universe, out-of-sample, after transaction costs, slippage, and Deflated Sharpe Ratio correction.** This design is deliberately narrow: it tests one well-evidenced mechanism (PEAD-adjacent drift) combined with one defensible ML approach (gradient boosting over engineered features, per Kelly-Malamud-Zhou), rather than stacking every available signal category and hoping something sticks. The backtest should draw historical price/volume bars from Alpaca's free tier (5+ years, thousands of symbols) cross-checked against `yfinance` for gap detection, and should source its stock universe from a point-in-time, survivorship-bias-free constituent list — either the free Koker/Arbi DIY approach or an EODHD subscription — specifically to avoid the silent inflation that "current S&P 500 list" backtests introduce. Validation must use walk-forward (not standard k-fold, which leaks future information) with a holdout period never touched during development, and any headline Sharpe ratio should be reported alongside its Deflated Sharpe Ratio and Probability of Backtest Overfitting equivalents rather than at face value, given how consistently uncorrected backtests in this exact literature have been shown to overstate significance. Cost modeling should include FINRA TAF and SEC Section 31 fees on sells, an assumed slippage figure in basis points (since no broker publishes its fill-simulation methodology in technical detail), and — critically — the T+1 settlement lag if a cash account is modeled, since a genuinely weekly-rebalanced multi-stock rotation can trigger good-faith violations if the simulation assumes instant reuse of sale proceeds. Only after a strategy clears walk-forward validation with cost and overfitting corrections intact should it move to Alpaca's paper-trading API for a live-data rehearsal — and even then, the PoC should treat paper P&L as a rehearsal of mechanics (order routing, position sizing, rebalance logic) rather than a promise of live profitability, since none of the brokers researched publish enough about their fill-simulation fidelity to treat paper returns as a reliable forecast. No real money should be involved at any stage of this first PoC.

## How to evaluate any third-party "it works" claim — including this project's own eventual one

Free (and paid) "AI stock prediction" products are common, and it's worth having a standing answer for how to evaluate them rather than re-litigating it each time one comes up: factor-breakdown scores (Danelfin's "AI Score," split into fundamental/technical/sentiment components; Seeking Alpha's Quant Ratings, split into growth/profitability/momentum/valuation/EPS revisions), pattern-recognition call-outs (Tickeron-style "detected a cup-and-handle pattern" tools), and a newer wave of LLM-generated narrative rationale ("AI stock analysis" chatbots that write a paragraph of "why") are all real products with real users. None of them clear a different evidentiary bar than this document applies to every signal category above, and most clear a much lower one. The prior should start skeptical rather than neutral, on simple economic logic: a product that reliably predicted market-beating moves would be worth more traded than given away free, so a free product's continued existence as a free product is itself mild evidence against the claim. The concrete failure modes match this document's own findings almost exactly: no independently audited, timestamped-in-advance track record; survivorship bias in which predictions stay visible (losing calls quietly age out of the marketing surface, winning ones get highlighted); and the identical overfitting fingerprint catalogued above — in-sample-looking-great, out-of-sample-collapsing results, reported against the vendor's own chosen baseline rather than an independent one.

One failure mode here is newer than the rest of this document and easy to underweight: **an explanation attached to a prediction does not validate it, and can make a bad prediction more persuasive without making it more correct.** This is sharpest for LLM-generated narrative rationale — a model can produce a fluent, confident-sounding paragraph of "why" regardless of whether the underlying signal is real, because generating plausible-sounding reasoning and generating accurate reasoning are different capabilities that don't necessarily travel together. This is the "statistics as decoration" failure mode named in this document's conclusion below, now showing up as a packaged product category rather than just an uncorrected backtest. A live example worth treating with the same skepticism: [krafercrypto.com's KAT models](https://krafercrypto.com/) publish bar-by-bar price predictions with reported accuracy against a stated random-walk baseline (e.g. "64.8% on BTC") — a better practice than a bare accuracy number, but still a self-reported figure against a self-chosen baseline, not an independently audited one (see [`prediction-approach.md`](prediction-approach.md) for more on this specific example, in the context of candle-level prediction as a modeling target).

The only legitimate test — for a third-party claim, or for this project's own eventual result — is the same one applied throughout this document: pull the predictions, timestamp them going forward rather than retroactively, and run an independent out-of-sample / walk-forward check before trusting any of it, this project's own included.

## Conclusion

The 2026 landscape has quietly removed most of the *structural* barriers to this kind of project — cheap fractional-share execution, a relaxed PDT regime, free paper trading, and a genuinely low-cost data stack — while leaving the *evidentiary* barrier almost exactly where it has been for years: no retail-accessible signal category has a well-replicated, cost-net track record, and the categories that look most promising on paper (LLM sentiment, complex ML models) are also the categories most prone to the specific kind of uncorrected-backtest inflation this research repeatedly documents. The more interesting shift is regulatory rather than technical: because paper trading and (mostly) cash-account trading now sit almost entirely outside the old day-trading rulebook, the actual gating question for a solo developer is no longer "can I legally do this with $500" but "can I build a backtest rigorous enough to trust its own conclusion" — which reframes this project correctly as a statistics and engineering exercise wearing a trading costume, not a trading strategy wearing statistics as decoration.
