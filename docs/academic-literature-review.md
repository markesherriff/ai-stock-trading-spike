# Real Edges Exist, But Vanish Fast

Six decades of peer-reviewed finance research converge on a single, unglamorous verdict: **statistically real predictability in stock returns does exist**, but it is small, concentrated in the least liquid corners of the market, shrinks the moment it becomes known, and almost never survives the trip from a backtest spreadsheet to a live brokerage account. Momentum (Jegadeesh & Titman, 1993), post-earnings-announcement drift (Ball & Brown, 1968; Bernard & Thomas, 1989), and machine-learning-detected nonlinear return patterns (Gu, Kelly & Xiu, 2020) are not fabrications — they are among the most replicated findings in empirical asset pricing. But every one of these threads is paired with an equally rigorous demonstration that the exploitable portion is far smaller than the discovery paper implies: transaction costs, capacity limits, arbitrage by better-capitalized players, and the sheer statistical hazard of testing thousands of strategy variants against the same historical data all eat into "predictability" long before it becomes "profit." The most damning single data point in this entire literature is not theoretical — it is empirical: a peer-reviewed study of 888 real, user-submitted trading algorithms found that a strategy's backtested Sharpe ratio has essentially **zero power to predict** its subsequent live performance (Wiecki, Campbell, Lent & Stauth, 2016). Layer on top of that three independent, cross-country studies of actual (non-backtested) trading records showing that the overwhelming majority of active retail traders — 80–97% depending on market and horizon — lose money net of costs, with **no measurable evidence that unprofitable traders learn to improve** (Barber & Odean, 2000; Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean, 2014; Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti, 2020), and the honest picture comes into focus: this is a field where the academic evidence for "something is there" is unusually strong by social-science standards, and the academic evidence for "you can extract it as a retail trader" is unusually weak. What follows catalogs that evidence paper by paper across four threads — technical analysis, ML/AI models, sentiment/NLP, and overfitting/real-world failure — before a closing synthesis addresses the project's central question directly.

## Thread 1: Technical analysis survives as statistical curiosity, not trading edge

The foundational efficient-markets literature set up technical analysis to fail by construction: if weak-form efficiency holds, past prices contain no information not already reflected in the current price, so chart patterns and moving-average rules should be worthless (Fama, 1970). Six decades of testing that premise produced a more complicated answer than either side of the debate usually admits. **Momentum and short-term reversal are the two anomalies that consistently survive rigorous, out-of-sample, cost-adjusted re-testing** — Jegadeesh and Titman's original 1993 finding of roughly 1%-per-month returns to buying past winners and selling past losers was re-tested by the same authors in 2001 on an entirely new decade of data and held up, with a statistically significant **~9.29% annual return surviving a 0.5% transaction-cost assumption** ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7159/w7159.pdf)). But rigorous, purpose-built 2023–2024 re-tests of the broader universe of classical technical rules — moving averages, trading-range breaks, oscillator-style signals — reach the opposite conclusion once data-snooping bias is properly corrected. Kevin Rink's test of **6,406 trading rules across 41 developed and emerging equity indices** found predictability "diminishes drastically over time in all markets" and is "highly sensitive to even moderate transaction costs," with out-of-sample results underperforming buy-and-hold ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11408-023-00433-2)). Chuang, Chuang, Wang and Xu ran the same exercise on **38,456 rules in the Chinese market** and found winning in-sample rules "turn net-negative out-of-sample once transaction costs are included" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927538X24000295)). Malkiel's own peer-reviewed contribution to this debate — not just his popular book — directly engaged the anomalies literature and concluded markets "are more efficient and less predictable than many recent academic papers would have us believe" ([AEA/JEP](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F089533003321164958)).

### Papers cataloged

- **Fama (1970), "Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work," *Journal of Finance*.** Foundational weak-form-efficiency paper; serial-correlation and filter-rule tests on Dow stocks (1957–1962) found negligible predictable structure — implying pure technical analysis on past prices should not generate abnormal risk-adjusted returns. Reputability: very high (basis of Fama's 2013 Nobel). ([Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/review/eugene-fama-efficient-markets-and-the-nobel-prize))
- **Fama (1998), "Market Efficiency, Long-Term Returns, and Behavioral Finance," *Journal of Financial Economics*.** Concludes long-term-return anomalies are about as often overreaction as underreaction and are largely consistent with efficiency as a working model. Reputability: very high. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0304405X98000269))
- **Fama & French (2008), "Dissecting Anomalies," *Journal of Finance*.** Tests major anomalies across firm sizes; finds momentum, share-issuance and accrual anomalies pervasive and real, while asset-growth/profitability anomalies are weaker and concentrated in micro-caps — a partial concession that some anomalies are not statistical artifacts. Reputability: very high. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01371.x))
- **Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000), "Foundations of Technical Analysis," *Journal of Finance*.** Using kernel regression on U.S. stocks (1962–1996), finds several chart patterns (head-and-shoulders, double tops, etc.) "provide incremental information and may have some practical value" — challenging strict weak-form efficiency, though the claim is about statistical information content, not proven net-of-cost profitability. Reputability: very high. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/0022-1082.00265); [NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7613/w7613.pdf))
- **Shleifer & Vishny (1997), "The Limits of Arbitrage," *Journal of Finance*.** Not about technical analysis directly, but the standard theoretical reason detectable mispricing can persist: real-world arbitrage needs capital and is risky, so mispricing isn't instantly competed away. Reputability: very high. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1540-6261.1997.tb03807.x))
- **McLean & Pontiff (2016), "Does Academic Research Destroy Stock Return Predictability?", *Journal of Finance*.** Studying 97 published return-predicting variables, finds returns **26% lower out-of-sample** and **58% lower post-publication**, with declines concentrated in high-idiosyncratic-risk, low-liquidity stocks — direct evidence that publicizing an anomaly shrinks it. Reputability: very high. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12365))
- **Jacobs & Müller (2020), "Anomalies across the Globe: Once Public, No Longer Existent?", *Journal of Financial Economics*.** Examining 138 anomalies across 39 markets, finds the **United States is the only country with a statistically meaningful post-publication decline**; internationally, anomaly returns show no reliable decay, arguing against a pure data-mining explanation and for genuine, arbitrage-limited mispricing. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2816490))
- **Jegadeesh & Titman (1993), "Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers," *Journal of Finance*.** Foundational momentum paper: 3–12 month winners-minus-losers strategies earned roughly 1%/month (1965–1989), not explained by standard risk factors. Reputability: very high. ([UH PDF](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/rsusmel/phd/jegadeesh-titman93.pdf))
- **Jegadeesh & Titman (2001), "Profitability of Momentum Strategies," *Journal of Finance*.** Re-tests momentum out-of-sample on 1990s data; profits persisted, and a 6-month/6-month strategy retained a statistically significant **~9.29%/year** return after a 0.5% transaction-cost assumption. Reputability: very high. ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w7159/w7159.pdf))
- **Daniel & Moskowitz (2016), "Momentum Crashes," *Journal of Financial Economics*.** Momentum strategies suffer rare, severe crashes (monthly losses up to ~90% in the worst cases) concentrated in post-decline, high-volatility "panic" periods; a dynamic strategy conditioning on forecasted crash risk roughly doubles the static strategy's alpha and Sharpe ratio. Reputability: very high. ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w20439/w20439.pdf))
- **de Groot, Huij & Zhou (2011), "Another Look at Trading Costs and Short-Term Reversal Profits," *Journal of Banking & Finance*.** Raw weekly reversal profits are mostly attributable to costly-to-trade small caps; restricted to large caps with turnover-reducing construction, reversal still nets roughly 30–50 basis points/week after realistic costs — real but much smaller and more fragile than raw numbers suggest. Reputability: very high. ([Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/25718/AnotherLook_2011.pdf))
- **Rink (2023), "The Predictive Ability of Technical Trading Rules," *Financial Markets and Portfolio Management*.** Tests 6,406 rules across 41 developed/emerging indices over up to 66 years with a data-snooping correction; finds predictability "diminishes drastically over time," is highly cost-sensitive, and recently-winning rules underperform buy-and-hold out-of-sample. Reputability: high. ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11408-023-00433-2))
- **Chuang, Chuang, Wang & Xu (2024), "Profitability of Technical Trading Rules in the Chinese Stock Market," *Pacific-Basin Finance Journal*.** Tests 38,456 rules on two Chinese indices (2010–2021); only a few complex rules beat one index in some sub-periods, none beat the other, and winning in-sample rules **turn net-negative out-of-sample** after costs. Reputability: high. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0927538X24000295))
- **Neely, Rapach, Tu & Zhou (2014), "Forecasting the Equity Risk Premium: The Role of Technical Indicators," *Management Science*.** At the aggregate-market level (not individual-stock alpha), technical indicators show statistically and economically significant in- and out-of-sample power to forecast the equity risk premium, matching or exceeding macro variables. Reputability: very high, but scope differs from the individual-security studies above. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1787554))
- **Malkiel (2003), "The Efficient Market Hypothesis and Its Critics," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*.** Peer-reviewed rebuttal to behavioral-finance critiques; concludes markets "are more efficient and less predictable than many recent academic papers would have us believe," while granting some patterns exist. Reputability: very high. ([AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2F089533003321164958))

### Where this thread disagrees with itself

The sharpest documented dispute is **McLean & Pontiff (2016) versus Jacobs & Müller (2020)**: the "academic publication destroys anomalies" story that dominates practitioner commentary turns out to be a largely U.S.-specific phenomenon, tied to how much liquid arbitrage capital is available to compete a mispricing away, not a universal law of markets. There is also a subtler, easily-conflated tension between **Neely, Rapach, Tu & Zhou (2014)**, who find technical indicators genuinely forecast the aggregate equity risk premium, and **Rink (2023)/Chuang et al. (2024)**, who find no exploitable edge in individual-stock or index technical trading rules — these are not strictly contradictory findings since they answer different questions (market-timing versus security-picking), but retail-facing commentary regularly cites the former as proof the latter works, which the evidence does not support. Finally, **Lo, Mamaysky & Wang (2000)** sits awkwardly inside the same journal (Journal of Finance) as Fama's own foundational efficiency work, finding real informational content in chart patterns that the strictest weak-form reading would predict shouldn't exist — a reminder that "efficient markets" was never a unanimous, closed academic question even among top-tier peer-reviewed authors.

## Thread 2: ML and AI models find genuine signal, then argue about why

Machine learning's contribution to return prediction is the one place in this literature where the top-tier peer-reviewed evidence for "this works" is unusually strong — and where the fiercest live methodological fight is currently happening. Gu, Kelly and Xiu's landmark *Review of Financial Studies* paper found nonlinear tree and neural-network models produce genuinely large economic gains over linear regression approaches, doubling the performance of leading factor strategies in some specifications, with all methods converging on momentum, liquidity and volatility as the dominant signals ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/33/5/2223/5758276)). Kelly, Malamud and Zhou followed with a *Journal of Finance* paper proving theoretically and showing empirically that "complex" models — those with more parameters than observations — can produce *higher* out-of-sample return predictability and Sharpe ratios than simple ones, directly contradicting the intuition that more parameters must mean more overfitting ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13298)). But 2025 has brought a direct, credible academic attack on the mechanism behind that second result: Stefan Nagel's NBER working paper argues the "virtue of complexity" is substantially a mechanical artifact of how the random-feature construction times market volatility, not a generic predictability gain ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34104/w34104.pdf)), a critique echoed by Buncic (2025) and Cartea & Shi (2025). Deep learning for sequence prediction tells an even more instructive story about decay: Fischer and Krauss's widely-cited LSTM paper found genuinely strong out-of-sample directional accuracy on S&P 500 constituents from 1992–2015 — but reported, in the same paper, that "excess returns seem to have been arbitraged away" after 2010, with post-cost profitability fluctuating around zero ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221717310652)). Reinforcement learning for trading is the least mature of the three: even the field's own review literature concludes that "for the majority of works reviewed, despite showing statistically significant improvements in performance compared to established baseline strategies, no decent profitability level was obtained" once realistic frictions are modeled ([arXiv 2106.00123](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00123)).

### Papers cataloged

- **Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020), "Empirical Asset Pricing via Machine Learning," *Review of Financial Studies* 33(5).** Trees and neural networks deliver large economic gains over linear models by capturing nonlinear predictor interactions; a neural-network-sorted long-short portfolio reportedly achieves an annualized out-of-sample Sharpe ratio near **1.35–1.45** (a figure corroborated by independent replications, not the primary table itself). Reputability: very high. ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/33/5/2223/5758276); [Tidy Finance replication](https://www.tidy-finance.org/blog/gu-kelly-xiu-replication/))
- **Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024), "The Virtue of Complexity in Return Prediction," *Journal of Finance* 79(1).** Proves and empirically documents that highly overparameterized ("complex") models generally increase return-prediction R² and portfolio Sharpe ratios versus simple models, largely by improving forecasts of market downturns/volatility timing. Reputability: very high, but actively contested (see below). ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13298))
- **Nagel (2025), "Seemingly Virtuous Complexity in Return Prediction," NBER Working Paper 34104.** Direct critique: argues the apparent complexity gains are partly a mechanical/volatility-timing artifact of the random-Fourier-feature construction rather than genuine economic predictability. Reputability: credible NBER working paper, not yet journal-published. ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w34104/w34104.pdf))
- **Israel, Kelly & Moskowitz (2020), "Can Machines 'Learn' Finance?", *Journal of Investment Management*** (2020 Harry M. Markowitz Award winner). Concludes ML for asset management faces distinct challenges (low signal-to-noise, limited/non-stationary data, adaptive markets) and that success requires combining ML with economic theory rather than off-the-shelf application. Reputability: high, peer-reviewed practitioner-academic journal, award-winning. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3624052))
- **Fischer & Krauss (2018), "Deep learning with long short-term memory networks for financial market predictions," *European Journal of Operational Research* 270(2).** LSTM networks on S&P 500 constituents (1992–2015) beat momentum and memory-free benchmarks, with pre-cost daily returns of ~0.46% and Sharpe ~5.8 — but "outperformance ... was very clear from 1992 to 2009 ... as of 2010, excess returns seem to have been arbitraged away," fluctuating around zero post-transaction-cost. Reputability: very high, one of the most-cited papers in this sub-field, and unusually honest about its own decay. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0377221717310652))
- **Sezer, Gudelek & Ozbayoglu (2020), "Financial time series forecasting with deep learning: A systematic literature review 2005–2019," *Applied Soft Computing*.** First comprehensive DL survey for financial forecasting; catalogs widespread use of RNN/LSTM architectures without asserting a robust, consistent live-trading edge across the reviewed literature. Reputability: very high, peer-reviewed. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1568494620301216))
- **Zhang, Zohren & Roberts (2020), "Deep Reinforcement Learning for Trading," *The Journal of Financial Data Science* 2(2).** DRL applied to 50 liquid futures contracts (2011–2019) "outperforms classical time-series momentum strategies, delivering positive profits despite heavy transaction costs" — one of the more credible RL claims because it explicitly models costs, though still a backtest, not live trading. Reputability: high, peer-reviewed practitioner-academic journal. ([JFDS](https://jfds.pm-research.com/content/early/2020/03/16/jfds.2020.1.030))
- **Yang, Liu, Zhong & Walid (2020), "Deep Reinforcement Learning for Automated Stock Trading: An Ensemble Strategy," ACM ICAIF.** An ensemble of PPO/A2C/DDPG on the 30 Dow stocks reports a backtested Sharpe ratio of 2.81 and 52.61% annual return versus the Dow index and a min-variance benchmark — backtest only, no live or paper-trading results disclosed. Reputability: high, peer-reviewed conference (widely cited, open-source FinRL implementation). ([ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3383455.3422540))
- **"Deep Reinforcement Learning in Quantitative Algorithmic Trading: A Review," arXiv 2106.00123 (2021).** Field-wide review conclusion: despite statistically significant improvements over baselines in most reviewed papers, "no decent profitability level was obtained" once realistic frictions are modeled; RL-for-trading remains "in very early stages of development" with no real-time trading-platform testing found across the literature. Reputability: moderate (unreviewed but widely referenced survey), notable for being self-critical of its own field. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.00123))

### Where this thread disagrees with itself

The central live dispute is **Kelly, Malamud & Zhou (2024) versus Nagel (2025), Buncic (2025), and Cartea & Shi (2025)**: a top-journal, theoretically rigorous claim that model complexity generically improves return prediction is now under direct, technically serious attack over whether the effect is genuine economic predictability or a mechanical byproduct of how the random-feature model construction interacts with volatility. As of this review, no published rebuttal from the original authors has appeared, so this should be read as **contested, not settled**, despite the strength of the original venue. A second, quieter tension sits inside the deep-learning literature: architecture comparisons circulating in recent (non-peer-reviewed) work claim transformers "consistently outperform" LSTMs on some metrics while simultaneously being flagged as "more sensitive to noise" and more overfitting-prone — results that are architecture- and dataset-specific rather than a clean verdict either way. Corroborating this thread's skeptical pole from outside the notes folder, a 2024 study running 13 ML models on SPY minute-bar data found directional accuracy collapsing from 80–86% in-sample to **47–49% out-of-sample** — worse than a coin flip — with 12 of 13 models underperforming buy-and-hold ([arXiv 2412.15448](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.15448v1)), a useful concrete illustration of exactly the in-sample/out-of-sample gap this thread's more careful papers describe abstractly.

## Thread 3: Sentiment and NLP signals decay exactly where they're found

The sentiment literature has the clearest, most consistent internal pattern of any thread reviewed here: every well-documented signal source — earnings-news pessimism, Twitter mood, StockTwits chatter, Reddit's WallStreetBets, and GPT-4-scored headlines — shows real predictive content when it is first discovered, and that content shrinks or disappears specifically where the signal becomes widely known, liquid, or heavily traded. Tetlock's Smith-Breeden Prize-winning *Journal of Finance* paper found media pessimism predicts downward price pressure followed by reversion to fundamentals ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2007.01232.x)), and his follow-up with Saar-Tsechansky and Macskassy found negative-word density in firm-specific news forecasts low earnings and a brief (not instant) stock-price under-reaction ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01362.x)) — both real effects, but explicitly short-horizon and modest. Post-earnings-announcement drift, the granddaddy of this literature (Ball & Brown, 1968; canonized by Bernard & Thomas, 1989), has been directly re-tested on modern data by Martineau (2022), who finds large-cap prices now fully reflect earnings surprises **on the announcement date itself**, with drift essentially gone for large stocks since **2006** and only recently disappearing even in microcaps ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3111607)) — a direct, modern confirmation that arbitrage speed has closed this window from the top of the market-cap distribution downward. Social media sentiment shows the same pattern with an added twist: the single most-cited paper in the space, Bollen, Mao & Zeng's "Twitter Mood Predicts the Stock Market" (2011), was directly and credibly discredited by an adversarial replication that found no true out-of-sample power and noted that a real hedge fund built on the idea, Derwent Capital Markets, failed and closed within roughly a year of launch ([Econ Journal Watch](https://econjwatch.org/file_download/1037/LachanskiPavSept2017.pdf)). Meanwhile Reddit's WallStreetBets offers the cleanest documented case of a real signal destroyed by its own popularity: pre-GameStop "Due Diligence" buy recommendations predicted meaningful one-month-ahead returns, a predictability that was **eliminated** once the platform's post-GameStop fame shifted its content toward attention-seeking posts (Bradley, Hanousek, Jame & Xiao, 2024, *Review of Financial Studies*) ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/37/5/1409/7486572)). The newest entrant, GPT-4-scored news headlines (Lopez-Lira & Tang, 2023–2025), is unusually candid about its own shelf life: it finds real next-day drift concentrated in small stocks and negative news, but explicitly reports that **strategy returns decline as LLM adoption rises** — the authors documenting their own edge being arbitraged away in close to real time ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07619)).

### Papers cataloged

- **Ball & Brown (1968), "An Empirical Evaluation of Accounting Income Numbers," *Journal of Accounting Research*.** First documented evidence that the market's price adjustment to earnings news continues to diverge for months after announcement — the origin of what became PEAD, though not originally framed as a trading anomaly. Reputability: very high, foundational. ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/joares/v6y1968i2p159-178.html))
- **Bernard & Thomas (1989), "Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift," *Journal of Accounting Research*.** Canonized PEAD: a standardized-unexpected-earnings decile long/short strategy generated **~18% annualized abnormal returns** (1974–1985), positive in 41 of 48 quarters, consistent with investor under-reaction rather than a risk premium. Reputability: very high. ([Caltech PDF](https://jkatz.caltech.edu/documents/28622/peads.pdf))
- **Chordia, Subrahmanyam & Tong (2014), "Have Capital Market Anomalies Attenuated...?", *Journal of Accounting and Economics*.** Anomaly-strategy returns (including PEAD-type strategies) roughly **halved after decimalization**, with daily profits essentially vanishing since the early 2000s, attributed to rising hedge-fund AUM, short interest and turnover. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2029057))
- **Martineau (2022), "Rest in Peace Post-Earnings-Announcement Drift," *Critical Finance Review*.** Modern re-test: large-cap prices now fully reflect earnings surprises on the announcement date; PEAD has been essentially non-existent for large caps since 2006, persisting longest (but now also disappearing) in microcaps. Reputability: very high, direct modern replication. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3111607))
- **Tetlock (2007), "Giving Content to Investor Sentiment," *Journal of Finance*** (2007 Smith-Breeden Prize). Media pessimism (WSJ's "Abreast of the Market" column) predicts downward price pressure followed by reversion to fundamentals, and predicts high trading volume — a market-level, not firm-level, finding. Reputability: very high, prize-winning. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2007.01232.x))
- **Tetlock, Saar-Tsechansky & Macskassy (2008), "More Than Words," *Journal of Finance*.** Negative-word density in firm-specific news forecasts low earnings, prices briefly under-react, and predictability is largest for fundamentals-focused stories. Reputability: very high. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1540-6261.2008.01362.x))
- **Huang, Wang & Yang (2023), "FinBERT: A Large Language Model for Extracting Information from Financial Text," *Contemporary Accounting Research*.** Their FinBERT variant substantially outperforms the Loughran-McDonald finance dictionary and standard ML baselines at sentiment classification — a measurement/tooling validation, not a returns-predictability backtest in itself. Reputability: high, peer-reviewed. ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1911-3846.12832))
- **Bollen, Mao & Zeng (2011), "Twitter Mood Predicts the Stock Market," *Journal of Computational Science*.** Claimed a Twitter-derived "calm" mood measure Granger-caused DJIA movements (2008) with reported ~87.6% directional accuracy; one of the most cited papers in this space (2,514+ citations within six years). Reputability at face value: high citation count, but see direct rebuttal below. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S187775031100007X))
- **Lachanski & Pav (2017), "Shy of the Character Limit... Revisited," *Econ Journal Watch*.** Direct adversarial replication: could not reproduce the original p-value pattern, found significance only in the original sub-sample (a data-snooping signature), and found **no out-of-sample predictive power**; notes the Twitter-mood-based hedge fund Derwent Capital Markets failed and closed in early 2012. Reputability: high, purpose-built replication journal. ([Econ Journal Watch](https://econjwatch.org/file_download/1037/LachanskiPavSept2017.pdf))
- **Renault (2017), "Intraday Online Investor Sentiment and Return Patterns," *Journal of Banking & Finance*.** StockTwits sentiment shifts during the first half-hour of trading forecast the S&P 500 ETF's return in the last half-hour of the *same day* — a genuine same-day intraday effect, driven specifically by novice/retail traders. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3010856))
- **Bradley, Hanousek, Jame & Xiao (2024), "Place Your Bets? ... Reddit's WallStreetBets," *Review of Financial Studies*.** Pre-GameStop, WSB "Due Diligence" buy recommendations predicted one-month-ahead returns and cash-flow news (an incremental recommendation associated with roughly a **5.17%** one-month-ahead return increase); post-GameStop, this predictability was **eliminated** as posts shifted toward attention-seeking content. Reputability: very high, top-tier journal. ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/37/5/1409/7486572))
- **Wang et al. (2022), "The Causal Relationship Between Social Media Sentiment and Stock Return," *Economics Letters*.** Experimental design finds message-board mood has a significant *causal* effect on same-day stock returns — a directionality claim that stands in tension with aggregator-reported findings that sentiment more often *follows* price than leads it. Reputability: high, peer-reviewed, though directionality in this literature remains genuinely split. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176522001793))
- **Lopez-Lira & Tang (2023–2025), "Can ChatGPT Forecast Stock Price Movements?", working paper (SSRN/arXiv 2304.07619).** Analyzing 50,000+ news headlines starting after ChatGPT's training cutoff, GPT-4 scores capture the same-day (largely non-tradable) reaction with ~90% portfolio-day hit rates **and** significantly predict next-day drift, especially in small stocks and negative news — but the authors explicitly report that **strategy returns decline as LLM adoption rises**, evidence of the edge being arbitraged away as the technique becomes known. Reputability: high-profile and methodologically careful, but still a working paper (not yet confirmed published in a top journal) — treat as a single, prominent study pending independent replication. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07619))
- Supplementary evidence (from the prior landscape survey, not the notes folder): a large-scale 2024 study of **965,375 U.S. news articles** found LLM-derived sentiment (OPT) reached **74.4% classification accuracy** versus 50.1% for the Loughran-McDonald dictionary, with backtested long-short Sharpe ratios up to 3.05 — explicitly *without* modeling transaction costs ([arXiv 2412.19245](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19245)), and same-day algorithmic news reactions have been measured completing within roughly **250 milliseconds**, faster than any human can register or act on ([arXiv 1408.3650](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1408.3650)).

### Where this thread disagrees with itself

The starkest reversal is **Bollen, Mao & Zeng (2011) versus Lachanski & Pav (2017)**: one of the most-cited papers in computational social science, still frequently invoked in retail-trading commentary, has been directly discredited by a rigorous replication that found the original result was a multiple-comparisons artifact — and the corroborating real-world evidence (a hedge fund built on the thesis failing within about a year) makes this one of the strongest "citation count is not validity" lessons in the whole literature. A second, less resolved disagreement concerns causality direction in social-media sentiment: **Wang et al. (2022)** report an experimentally-verified causal effect of sentiment on same-day returns, while other aggregator-reported findings (not independently pinned to one primary source in this review) claim the reverse — that sentiment more often follows price moves than predicts them. Both directions plausibly operate simultaneously depending on platform and horizon, and this review could not locate one authoritative primary source resolving it cleanly.

## Thread 4: Overfitting research explains why backtests lie and traders lose

This is the most methodologically decisive thread in the literature, because it does not argue about whether any particular strategy works — it explains, mathematically and empirically, why almost any backtest-derived claim should be distrusted by default. Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu formalized "backtest overfitting" as a condition where picking the best-performing configuration from many trials predictably produces a strategy that performs *below* the median out-of-sample, and built a method (combinatorially symmetric cross-validation) to estimate the probability of it happening ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253)). Their companion Deflated Sharpe Ratio paper shows a headline Sharpe ratio has to be discounted by a function of how many strategy variants were tried and how skewed the return distribution is — information vendors and fund marketing materials essentially never disclose ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551)). The empirical proof that this isn't just theoretical hand-wringing comes from Wiecki, Campbell, Lent and Stauth's peer-reviewed study of 888 real, user-submitted algorithmic strategies on the Quantopian platform, tracked for at least six months of genuine live performance: standard backtest metrics, including the Sharpe ratio, had **essentially no power to predict** subsequent live performance (R² < 0.025), and the more a quant had iterated on a strategy, the *larger* the gap between its backtest and its live result tended to be ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2745220)). A parallel, unresolved academic fight concerns the "factor zoo": Harvey, Liu and Zhu's *Review of Financial Studies* paper argues that with hundreds of published factors already tested, the traditional significance bar is far too lax and should be raised from a t-statistic of ~2.0 to ~3.0, implying many published anomalies are likely false positives ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2249314)) — but Andrew Chen's more recent false-discovery-rate analysis of the same broad literature concludes the false-discovery rate is likely **≤25%, possibly ≤9%**, the opposite of the popular "most factors are noise" reading ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15365)). Underneath all of this sits the bleakest and most consistent empirical finding in the entire report: three independent studies, in three countries, using real (not backtested) brokerage or regulator transaction records, find the overwhelming majority of active retail and day traders lose money net of costs, with essentially no evidence that losers learn to improve.

### Papers cataloged

- **Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2017), "The Probability of Backtest Overfitting," *Journal of Computational Finance*.** Defines backtest overfitting formally and introduces CSCV to numerically estimate its probability; shows the probability of selecting an overfit strategy grows rapidly with the number of trials, and that standard regression anti-overfitting techniques are unreliable for investment backtests specifically. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2326253))
- **Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado & Zhu (2014), "Pseudo-Mathematics and Financial Charlatanism," *Notices of the American Mathematical Society*.** Proves high simulated performance is easily achievable after testing a small number of alternative configurations, and that the more configurations tried, the greater the probability the backtest is overfit — argues investors "cannot evaluate the degree of overfitting in most investment proposals" because the number of trials is never disclosed. Reputability: very high, aimed at mathematicians rather than finance practitioners specifically to flag misuse of mathematical rigor. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2308659))
- **Bailey & López de Prado (2014), "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio," *Journal of Portfolio Management* 40(5).** Formalizes a correction to the Sharpe ratio for selection bias (many trials, only the best reported) and non-normal returns, producing a probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds zero rather than a single fixed discount. Reputability: very high, highly cited practitioner-academic journal. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551))
- **Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), "…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns," *Review of Financial Studies*** (Editor's Choice). Argues the traditional significance threshold (t > 2.0) is far too lax given hundreds of already-tested factors, and recommends a hurdle near t > 3.0 — implying a large share of the published factor literature is likely false positive. Reputability: very high, top-3 finance journal. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2249314))
- **Chen (2021/2022), "Most Claimed Statistical Findings in Cross-Sectional Return Predictability Are Likely True," arXiv 2206.15365.** Directly re-examines Harvey-Liu-Zhu's own type of data using a large open-source dataset of 205+ published predictors and refined false-discovery-rate estimators; concludes the false discovery rate is bounded at ≤25% (possibly ≤9% under a refined bound) — the opposite conclusion from the popular "most factors are false" reading. Reputability: rigorous working paper (Federal Reserve Board-affiliated author), not yet confirmed journal-published, but a serious, data-driven rebuttal. ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.15365))
- **Wiecki, Campbell, Lent & Stauth (2016), "All That Glitters Is Not Gold," *Journal of Investing*.** Empirically tests 888 real user-submitted trading algorithms (Quantopian) against ≥6 months of subsequent live performance; standard backtest metrics including Sharpe ratio have essentially **no power to predict** out-of-sample performance (R² < 0.025), and more backtesting/iteration correlates with a *larger* backtest-to-live gap; ML models built on richer backtest features did better (out-of-sample Sharpe ~1.2), but this still illustrates how weak the raw backtest-to-live relationship is. Reputability: very high — the single most directly damning empirical data point in this review. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2745220))
- **Arian, Norouzi Mobarekeh & Seco (2024), "Backtest Overfitting in the Machine Learning Era," *Knowledge-Based Systems*.** Builds a synthetic market environment to stress-test out-of-sample validation methods under realistic ML-era conditions (non-stationarity, regime shifts); finds Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation outperforms plain K-Fold and Walk-Forward validation on measured overfitting risk — implying ML strategies are at least as overfitting-prone as classical ones, and that most retail/practitioner tooling (naive walk-forward) is not the best available defense. Reputability: high, peer-reviewed, directly extends the López de Prado toolkit into the ML era. ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0950705124011110))
- **Barber & Odean (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth," *Journal of Finance*.** Using 66,465 U.S. discount-broker households (1991–1996): the most active traders earned **11.4%** annually versus **17.9%** for the market, the gap attributed to trading costs and overconfidence-driven excess trading. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=219228))
- **Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (2009), "Just How Much Do Individual Investors Lose by Trading?", *Review of Financial Studies*.** Using Taiwan's full regulator-level investor population: aggregate individual trading produces an annual performance penalty of ~3.8 percentage points, closely mirrored by a ~1.5 point institutional gain — a near-direct wealth transfer. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=529062))
- **Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean (2014), "The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill," *Journal of Financial Markets*.** Taiwan day-trading data (1992–2006): **less than 1%** of the entire day-trading population predictably earns positive abnormal returns net of fees; the top 500 traders earned 37.9 bps/day after fees while the bottom-ranked lost -28.9 bps/day. Reputability: very high. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=529063))
- **Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti (2020), "Day Trading for a Living?", SSRN.** Using Brazilian securities-regulator records covering essentially the full population of new futures day traders (2013–2015): among those who persisted more than 300 days, **97% lost money**; only ~1.1% earned more than minimum wage from day trading; regression analysis found **no evidence traders improve with more experience**. Reputability: very high, regulator-level population data. ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3423101))
- **Elton, Gruber & Blake (1996), "Survivor Bias and Mutual Fund Performance," *Review of Financial Studies*.** Reconstructs the full 1976 mutual fund universe including funds that later merged out of existence; produces one of the first precise, primary-source estimates that survivorship bias inflates measured average fund returns. Reputability: very high, foundational and heavily cited. ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article-abstract/9/4/1097/1580100))

### Where this thread disagrees with itself

The clearest live academic dispute is **Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) versus Chen (2021/2022)**: both examine essentially the same universe of published return-predicting factors, and reach opposite headline conclusions about how much of it is false discovery — a raised-bar warning versus a more forgiving false-discovery-rate bound. Every party agrees the naive single-test significance threshold is inadequate; they disagree sharply on how bad the resulting damage actually is, and this review found no evidence the dispute has been resolved as of late 2026. Separately, it is worth flagging what this thread's own internal logic implies about **itself**: the Bailey/Borwein/López de Prado/Zhu program is a single, tightly linked author group producing mutually reinforcing papers (PBO, DSR, the AMS piece) rather than several fully independent teams converging on the same number — the mathematical argument is sound and now widely adopted (CPCV, DSR are industry-standard concepts), but the "how common is this in practice, quantitatively, across the real industry" claim rests more on this one group's framework than on a broad independent base-rate study.

## Grand Conclusion

Has this "largely been tried and found not to work"? Mostly, yes — but with an honest and important asterisk. The peer-reviewed literature across all four threads does not support the strong claim that markets are perfectly efficient and no signal has ever existed; momentum, short-horizon reversal, post-earnings drift in its early era, ML-detected nonlinear factor combinations, and freshly-discovered sentiment sources (WallStreetBets pre-2021, GPT-4 news scoring pre-widespread-adoption) were all, at some point, genuinely real and statistically documented in top-tier venues. What the literature supports overwhelmingly is a second, harder claim: **every one of those edges is a wasting asset**, and the rate of decay scales directly with how much liquidity, institutional attention, or technique-adoption the signal attracts — a pattern this review found independently replicated across PEAD (Martineau, 2022), LLM-news signals (Lopez-Lira & Tang), and social-forum sentiment (Bradley et al., 2024) using entirely different data, methods, and eras. Combine that decay dynamic with the two most empirically damning findings in the whole catalog — that backtested Sharpe ratios have essentially no power to predict live performance across 888 real algorithms (Wiecki et al., 2016), and that 80–97% of real, non-backtested retail and day traders lose money net of costs with no measurable learning curve (Barber & Odean; Barber, Lee, Liu & Odean; Chague, De-Losso & Giovannetti) — and the realistic prior for any single retail-built strategy, however well-motivated by the academic literature, should be firmly skeptical rather than optimistic. There is no "already-working solution" sitting in the academic record waiting to be implemented: even the field's most credible current claims (Kelly-Malamud-Zhou's complexity result, GKX's ML factor gains) are backtest-only, database-derived, and — in the KMZ case — under live, unresolved methodological attack as this report is written. That said, "mostly doesn't work" is not the same as "not worth trying." The literature also identifies exactly where the thinnest remaining ice is — small-cap, low-analyst-coverage names reacting to negative news or earnings surprises over a next-day-to-next-week window, and rigorously validated nonlinear combinations of conventional factors rather than single indicators — which gives a disciplined solo project a genuine, falsifiable hypothesis to test rather than a blank hope. The correct frame for this project, confirmed rather than merely suggested by sixty years of research, is a statistics experiment that happens to be denominated in dollars: worth running precisely because the literature is honest about how hard it is to win, and because the discipline of holding a strategy to walk-forward validation, deflated-Sharpe correction, and realistic transaction costs — the exact bar this literature says almost nobody clears — is itself the most valuable and most transferable output of the exercise, whether or not any specific signal survives contact with a live account.
